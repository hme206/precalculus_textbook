CH 1.4 – Arithmetic Combinations & Function Composition

Many times in the world of functions we need to be able to put more than one function together. This can occur for a number of real-world applications. Let's pretend that you are looking to start a new business selling personalized 32 oz. tumblers. You've been told that the cost to produce \(x\) tumblers can be modeled using the function \(p(x) = x^2 + \$30\), and you've also been told that the cost to personalize each one is \(c(x) = 2x + \$150\). As an entrepreneur you need to know the total cost for the entire tumbler, that means you'd need to put together the cost to make the tumblers and the cost to personalize them. In this case we'd add the two equations together using what we call arithmetic combinations.

Arithmetic Combinations

Arithmetic Combinations

Allow \(f\) and \(g\) to be two functions, such that their domains overlap each other.

  1. Sum: \((f+g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)\)
  2. Difference: \((f-g)(x) = f(x) - g(x)\)
  3. Product: \((fg)(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x)\)
  4. Quotient: \(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\), where \(g(x) \neq 0\)

Example

Using \(f(x) = x + 2\) and \(g(x) = x^2 + x\), find the arithmetic combinations below:

a.) \((f+g)(x)\) b.) \((fg)(3)\) c.) \((f-g)(2s)\)

Solution

a.)

\[ \begin{aligned} (f+g)(x) &= f(x) + g(x) \\ &= (x+2) + (x^2+x) \\ &= x + 2 + x^2 + x \\ &= x^2 + 2x + 2 \end{aligned} \]

b.)

\[ \begin{aligned} (fg)(3) &= f(3) \cdot g(3) \\ &= (3+2)(3^2+3) \\ &= 5 \cdot 12 \\ &= 60 \end{aligned} \]

c.)

\[ \begin{aligned} (f-g)(2s) &= f(2s) - g(2s) \\ &= (2s+2) - \bigl((2s)^2 + 2s\bigr) \\ &= 2s + 2 - 4s^2 - 2s \\ &= -4s^2 + 2 \end{aligned} \]

Function Compositions

Another way to put two functions together is by making a composition of two functions. In this type of operation you are placing one function into another function to make one function. Compositions of functions can be denoted by using a small circle that looks like a multiplication symbol "\(\circ\)" and can look like \((f \circ g)(x)\), which means "f composed with g".

Compositions

The composition of two functions \(f\) and \(g\) is:

\[(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))\]

The domain of the composition of \(f\) and \(g\) is the set of all \(x\) values in the domain of \(g\) such that \(g(x)\) is in the domain of \(f\).

An example for the domain of \((f \circ g)(x)\) would be if the domain of \(g(x) = \{1,2,3,4,5\}\) and the domain of \(f(x) = \{3,4,5,6\}\), then the domain of the composition would be \(\{3,4,5\}\) — only the values that \(g\) can accept and whose outputs land inside the domain of \(f\). Now, clearly we are dealing with continuous functions, meaning that our domains are intervals instead of sets. But this gives you an idea of how it works.

Example

Allow \(f(x) = 2x + 3\) and \(g(x) = x^2 - 3\). Find the following compositions below, then identify the domain of the composite function.

a.) \((f \circ g)(x)\) b.) \(g(f(x))\) c.) \((f \circ f)(3)\)

Solution

a.) To take the composition of \((f \circ g)(x)\), you take the function of \(g(x)\) and plug it into \(f(x)\) everywhere you see an \(x\).

\[ \begin{aligned} (f \circ g)(x) &= f\!\left(\textcolor{red}{g(x)}\right) \\ &= f\!\left(\textcolor{red}{x^2-3}\right) \\ &= 2\!\left(\textcolor{red}{x^2-3}\right)+3 \\ &= 2x^2 - 6 + 3 \\ &= 2x^2 - 3 \end{aligned} \]

To identify the domain of this composite function, we know that \(f(x)\) is a linear function with domain \((-\infty,\infty)\), and \(g(x)\) is a quadratic function which also has a domain of \((-\infty,\infty)\). Since both functions accept all real numbers, the domain of the composite function is \((-\infty,\infty)\).

b.) To take the composition of \(g(f(x))\), you take the function of \(f(x)\) and plug it into \(g(x)\) everywhere you see an \(x\).

\[ \begin{aligned} g(f(x)) &= g\!\left(\textcolor{red}{f(x)}\right) \\ &= g\!\left(\textcolor{red}{2x+3}\right) \\ &= \left(\textcolor{red}{2x+3}\right)^2 - 3 \\ &= (4x^2 + 12x + 9) - 3 \\ &= 4x^2 + 12x + 6 \end{aligned} \]

Once again, to identify the domain of this composite function, we know that \(f(x)\) is a linear function with domain \((-\infty,\infty)\), and \(g(x)\) is a quadratic function which also has a domain of \((-\infty,\infty)\). The domain of the composite function is therefore \((-\infty,\infty)\).

c.) To take the composition of \((f \circ f)(3)\), you take the function of \(f(3)\) and plug it into \(f(x)\) everywhere you see an \(x\). That means you first need to find \(f(3)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f(3) &= 2(\textcolor{red}{3}) + 3 \\ &= 6 + 3 \\ &= 9 \end{aligned} \]

Now plug that value into \(f(x)\), since this was \((f \circ f)(3)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} (f \circ f)(3) &= f\!\left(\textcolor{red}{f(3)}\right) \\ &= f\!\left(\textcolor{red}{9}\right) \\ &= 2(\textcolor{red}{9}) + 3 \\ &= 18 + 3 \\ &= 21 \end{aligned} \]

For this composition we ended up with a single value rather than a function, which means there is no function domain to identify.

The Difference Quotient

The difference quotient is an extremely important component of calculus. It is essentially the process we use to find something called a derivative. But don't worry — we aren't doing derivatives in this course. In short, the derivative of a function gives you the slope of a tangent line at a given point.

Difference Quotient

\[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}, \quad \text{where } h \neq 0\]

Example

a.) Use the difference quotient to find the slope of the tangent line of \(f(x) = 2x + 3\).

b.) Use the difference quotient to find the slope of the tangent line of \(f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 7\).

Solution

a.) This is essentially a multi-part combination. One way to make this slightly less confusing is to find \(f(x+h)\) first by substituting \((x+h)\) into \(f(x)\) everywhere you see an \(x\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x+h) &= 2(x+h) + 3 \\ &= 2x + 2h + 3 \end{aligned} \]

Next, put what you just found and \(f(x)\) into the difference quotient.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} &= \frac{(2x+2h+3)-(2x+3)}{h} \\ &= \frac{2x+2h+3-2x-3}{h} \\ &= \frac{2h}{h} \\ &= 2 \end{aligned} \]

As we can see, the slope of the line is two. This should have been something we recognized before we even applied the difference quotient — when a linear equation is written in slope-intercept form, the coefficient of \(x\) is always the slope.

b.) This one is a bit trickier, as we aren't dealing with a linear function but a quadratic. We still take the same steps. Start by finding \(f(x+h)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x+h) &= (x+h)^2 - 8(x+h) + 7 \\ &= x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - 8x - 8h + 7 \end{aligned} \]

Now plug that into the difference quotient.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} &= \frac{(x^2+2xh+h^2-8x-8h+7)-(x^2-8x+7)}{h} \\ &= \frac{x^2+2xh+h^2-8x-8h+7-x^2+8x-7}{h} \\ &= \frac{2xh+h^2-8h}{h} \\ &= \frac{h(2x-8+h)}{h} \\ &= 2x - 8 + h, \quad h \neq 0 \end{aligned} \]

Notice how the final answer still contains an \(h\) and is not a single number. That is because the value of the slope depends on where you are located on the parabola. This is a topic you will explore further in calculus.
