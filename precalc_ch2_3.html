CH 2.3 – Zeros of Polynomial Functions

Thus far we've looked at how to write a polynomial given its factors or zeros, and how to determine if something is a zero or factor of a given polynomial. Next we are going to learn how to find all zeros of a polynomial and how to completely factor one. This is going to require the information that we just went over in the previous chapters as well as a few new rules.

Carl Friedrich Gauss is a famous mathematician known for many different theorems and rules throughout mathematics. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra was one of them. He was one of the first to prove this theorem to be true.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

If \(f(x)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n\), where \(n > 0\), then \(f\) has at least one zero in the complex number system.

More precisely what this means is that in the complex number system a polynomial of degree \(n\) has exactly \(n\) zeros. Now keep in mind that just because we are in the complex number system doesn't mean that we can't have a real number as well. Technically speaking the zero of \(x = 4\) would be the imaginary number \(x = 4 + 0i\) where the imaginary part of the number is zero. Remember that all complex numbers have both a real part \((a)\) and an imaginary part \((bi)\).

With the use of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra and the equivalence of factors and zeros we obtain the Linear Factorization Theorem.

The Linear Factorization Theorem

If \(f(x)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n\), where \(n > 0\), then \(f(x)\) has exactly \(n\) linear factors:

\[f(x) = a_n(x - c_1)(x - c_2) \cdots (x - c_n)\]

where \(c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n\) are all complex numbers.

Both of these theorems are great because they tell you that zeros exist in all polynomials. What they don't tell you is how to actually find the zeros of a polynomial. When it comes to finding zeros of a polynomial there are a number of other tests and rules that can be applied. Frequently when trying to determine the zeros of a polynomial it is helpful to know how many of each type it could possibly have. For example if a polynomial only has negative zeros you don't want to be looking at positive zeros. Descartes' Rule of Signs tells us how many positive, negative, and imaginary zeros are possible in a given polynomial based on the sign variations within the polynomial.

Descartes' Rule of Signs

Allow \(f(x)\) to be a polynomial with real coefficients.

  1. The number of positive real zeros of \(f\) is either equal to the number of variations in sign changes of \(f(x)\) or less than that number by increments of two.
  2. The number of negative real zeros of \(f\) is either equal to the number of variations in sign changes of \(f(-x)\) or less than that number by increments of two.

When using Descartes' Rule it is important to remember that a polynomial will have a total of \(n\) zeros. That means if \(n = 5\) and you found that it had two positive zeros and one negative zero, that means you would have possibly two imaginary zeros to make the total five. Your total number of zeros still has to equal \(n\).

Descartes' Rule of Signs

Example

Determine the possible numbers of positive, negative, and imaginary zeros that \(f(x) = 3x^3 - 5x^2 + 6x - 4\) could have.

Solution

We first need to look at the sign changes in \(f(x)\) to determine the possible number of positive real zeros.

\[f(x) = \textcolor{blue}{+}3x^3 \textcolor{red}{-} 5x^2 \textcolor{blue}{+} 6x \textcolor{red}{-} 4\]

As we can see above the signs change from + to − a total of three times, that means that our polynomial could have either three positive real zeros or \((3 - 2 = 1)\) one positive real zero.

Next to determine the number of negative real zeros we need to first evaluate \(f(-x)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-x) &= 3(-x)^3 - 5(-x)^2 + 6(-x) - 4 \\ &= 3(-x^3) - 5(x^2) - 6x - 4 \\ &= \textcolor{red}{-}3x^3 \textcolor{red}{-} 5x^2 \textcolor{red}{-} 6x \textcolor{red}{-} 4 \end{aligned} \]

Notice how the sign does not change at all in \(f(-x)\); they are all negative. This means that there are no negative real zeros in this polynomial.

If we organized this information into a table we'd see that:
Positive 3 1
Negative 0 0
Imaginary 0 2
Total 3 3

Notice how in each column we end up with three total zeros. This table helps us to put the type of zeros that \(f(x)\) could have into a better setup. From looking at this we know that when trying to find zeros we don't need to try any negative numbers since this polynomial will have no negative real numbers.

Sometimes it is not that straightforward. For example, if \(f(x)\) is of degree \(n = 7\) and you find using Descartes' Rule that \(f(x)\) could have at most three positive and at most two negative real zeros, then our table would look something like this:

Positive 3 1 3 1
Negative 2 2 0 0
Imaginary 2 4 4 6
Total 7 7 7 7

We have to make sure to account for all the combinations of numbers that are possible. As you can see our bottom row still all adds up to seven since that was how many zeros this polynomial would have. It is also important to remember that imaginary zeros always come in pairs, so they should always have an even number.

The Rational Root Theorem

This rule can come in very handy especially when using the Rational Root Theorem, also sometimes called the Rational Zero Test. It all really just depends on the author and the book as to which wording they prefer. The Rational Root Theorem is helpful when solving or factoring polynomials because it helps identify possible rational zeros that satisfy the polynomial. It doesn't give us the exact zeros but it helps us to narrow them down. Then if you combine it with Descartes' Rule, it helps to narrow down the possible rational zeros even more. This sometimes is really nice, because the list of possible rational zeros the Rational Root Theorem helps you find can be extensive.

The Rational Root Theorem

Allow \(f(x)\) to be a polynomial with real coefficients, where \(a_n\) is the leading coefficient and \(a_0\) is the constant term, and they are both integers. Every rational zero of \(f\) has the form:

\[\text{Rational zero} = \frac{p}{q}\]

where \(p\) and \(q\) have no common factors other than 1, and:

  • \(p =\) a factor of the constant term \((a_0)\)
  • \(q =\) a factor of the leading coefficient \((a_n)\)

Example

Use the Rational Root Theorem to find all possible rational zeros of \(f(x) = 2x^3 + 3x^2 - 8x + 3\).

Solution

To identify all of the possible rational zeros of \(f(x)\) we first need to identify \(a_n\) and \(a_0\).

\(a_n = 2\)     Factors of \(a_n\), \(q = \pm 1, \pm 2\)

\(a_0 = 3\)     Factors of \(a_0\), \(p = \pm 1, \pm 3\)

To find the possible zeros you could create a table to help you organize the information, but it isn't necessary.

\(\dfrac{p}{q}\) \(\pm 1\) \(\pm 3\)
\(\pm 1\) \(\pm 1\) \(\pm 3\)
\(\pm 2\) \(\pm \dfrac{1}{2}\) \(\pm \dfrac{3}{2}\)

This tells us that our possible rational zeros of \(f(x)\) are:

\[\frac{p}{q} = \pm 1,\ \pm 3,\ \pm \frac{1}{2},\ \pm \frac{3}{2}\]

If we wanted to find all of the actual rational zeros of \(f(x)\) we'd need to check each of those zeros using synthetic division to see if they work or not. This example provided us with eight possible rational zeros, which is not a lot compared to some. This is why Descartes' Rule is nice, because it could tell us that we don't need to check for any negative real zeros, which would cut down to four zeros that we'd have to check using synthetic division.

Upper and Lower Bound Rules

Thus far we've started to put together the process for finding zeros of a polynomial function. As we've seen, some of the rules help us in trying to identify those zeros. The next rule we are going to look at helps you to identify if the number you're trying is an upper bound or a lower bound for our polynomial.

Upper and Lower Bound Rules

Allow \(f(x)\) to be a polynomial with a positive leading coefficient \(a_n\). If when dividing \(f(x)\) by \((x - c)\) using synthetic division, then:

  1. If \(c > 0\), and each number in the last row is positive or zero, then \(c\) is an upper bound. Meaning there is no zero higher than that number.
  2. If \(c < 0\), and each number in the last row alternates between positive and negative, then \(c\) is a lower bound. Meaning there is no zero below that number.

Now, if we combine together all of these rules and tests we can start to begin the process of finding all zeros of a polynomial function.

Example

Find all zeros of the function \(f(x) = 8x^3 - 4x^2 + 6x - 3\).

Solution

To begin this process we're going to start with Descartes' Rule.

Start by finding the number of sign changes in \(f(x)\):

\[f(x) = \textcolor{blue}{+}8x^3 \textcolor{red}{-} 4x^2 \textcolor{blue}{+} 6x \textcolor{red}{-} 3\]

This means there are three sign changes in \(f(x)\) suggesting there are either three or one positive zeros.

Next identify the sign changes in \(f(-x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-x) &= 8(-x)^3 - 4(-x)^2 + 6(-x) - 3 \\ &= \textcolor{red}{-}8x^3 \textcolor{red}{-} 4x^2 \textcolor{red}{-} 6x \textcolor{red}{-} 3 \end{aligned} \]

This means that there are no negative zeros.
Positive31
Negative00
Imaginary02
Total33

Next we move on to our Rational Root Theorem (\(p\) & \(q\)'s):

\(a_n = 8\)     Factors of \(a_n\), \(q = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4, \pm 8\)

\(a_0 = 3\)     Factors of \(a_0\), \(p = \pm 1, \pm 3\)

This means our possible zeros would be:

\[\frac{p}{q} = \pm 1,\ \pm 3,\ \pm \frac{1}{2},\ \pm \frac{1}{4},\ \pm \frac{1}{8},\ \pm \frac{3}{2},\ \pm \frac{3}{4},\ \pm \frac{3}{8}\]

Now we are ready to start and use synthetic division to determine which of these zeros may be our solutions.

Testing \(x = \dfrac{3}{4}\):

\(\frac{3}{4}\) \(8\) \(-4\) \(6\) \(-3\)
\(6\) \(\frac{3}{2}\) \(\frac{57}{8}\)
\(8\) \(2\) \(\frac{15}{2}\) \(\frac{33}{8}\)
Now notice how the last row of our synthetic division is all positive values. This means that since \(\frac{3}{4} > 0\) then \(x = \frac{3}{4}\) is an upper bound of our polynomial. Meaning there is no need to check any of our possible rational zeros that are greater than \(\frac{3}{4}\).

Next we will try our \(x = \frac{1}{2}\) zero and see if that works.

Testing \(x = \dfrac{1}{2}\):

\(\frac{1}{2}\) \(8\) \(-4\) \(6\) \(-3\)
\(4\) \(0\) \(3\)
\(8\) \(0\) \(6\) \(0\)
Recall that if the last row ends in a zero then it is a zero of the polynomial \(f(x)\).

Now we can take our remaining polynomial and solve it for \(x\) since we have reduced it to a quadratic.

\[ \begin{aligned} 8x^2 + 6 &= 0 \\ 8x^2 &= -6 \\ x^2 &= \frac{-6}{8} \\ x &= \pm\sqrt{\frac{-3}{4}} \\ x &= \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i \end{aligned} \]

This means that our zeros for \(f(x)\) are equal to:

\[\left\{\frac{1}{2},\ -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i,\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i\right\}\]

With a factored form of:

\[f(x) = \left(x - \frac{1}{2}\right)\left(x + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i\right)\left(x - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i\right)\]

The Full Process for Finding Zeros

Now that we've gone through the entire process to solve a polynomial function let's set out a set of steps.

Steps for Finding All Zeros of a Polynomial

  1. Apply Descartes' Rule of Signs.
  2. Apply the Rational Root Theorem.
  3. Use synthetic division with upper/lower bounds rules to identify all rational zeros of the polynomial \(f(x)\).
  4. When applicable, solve the remaining quadratic to find imaginary or irrational zeros.
  5. Write out zeros using set notation.
  6. Check our solutions by setting up the polynomial in factored form and multiplying it all back out.

Example

Solve \(f(x) = x^4 + x^3 - 16x^2 - 4x + 48\). Then write \(f(x)\) as a product of its linear factors.

Solution

Step 1: Descartes' Rule of Signs

Number of sign changes in \(f(x)\):

\[f(x) = \textcolor{blue}{+}x^4 \textcolor{blue}{+} x^3 \textcolor{red}{-} 16x^2 \textcolor{red}{-} 4x \textcolor{blue}{+} 48\]

As we can see there are two sign changes meaning there are two positive zeros or less than that by two for this polynomial.

Number of sign changes in \(f(-x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-x) &= (-x)^4 + (-x)^3 - 16(-x)^2 - 4(-x) + 48 \\ &= \textcolor{blue}{+}x^4 \textcolor{red}{-} x^3 \textcolor{red}{-} 16x^2 \textcolor{blue}{+} 4x \textcolor{blue}{+} 48 \end{aligned} \]

From this we can see that there are two sign changes. This means there are two negative zeros, or less than that by two.

Step 2: Rational Root Theorem

\(a_n = 1\)     Factors of \(a_n\), \(q = \pm 1\)

\(a_0 = 48\)     Factors of \(a_0\), \(p = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6, \pm 8, \pm 12, \pm 16, \pm 24, \pm 48\)

\[\frac{p}{q} = \pm 1,\ \pm 2,\ \pm 3,\ \pm 4,\ \pm 6,\ \pm 8,\ \pm 12,\ \pm 16,\ \pm 24,\ \pm 48\]

Notice the alternating signs and \(c < 0\) — that means it's a lower bound.

Testing \(x = -4\):

\(-4\) \(1\) \(1\) \(-16\) \(-4\) \(48\)
\(-4\) \(12\) \(16\) \(-48\)

This is a zero!!

Testing \(x = -2\) on the reduced polynomial \(x^3 - 3x^2 - 4x + 12\):

We can see that this is also a zero. Notice that we did not use the original equation for the synthetic division — we used the quotient from the previous problem to evaluate this one. Now that we're down to a quadratic we will proceed to Step 4.

Step 4: Solve the remaining quadratic by factoring

\[ \begin{aligned} x^2 - 5x + 6 &= 0 \\ (x - 3)(x - 2) &= 0 \\ x &= 3,\ 2 \end{aligned} \]

Step 5: Zeros and factored form

This means that our zeros are \(\{-4,\ -2,\ 2,\ 3\}\) and our factored form would be:

\[f(x) = (x + 4)(x + 2)(x - 2)(x - 3)\]

Step 6: Check

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= (x + 4)(x + 2)(x - 2)(x - 3) \\ &= (x^2 + 6x + 8)(x^2 - 5x + 6) \\ &= x^4 + x^3 - 16x^2 - 4x + 48 \end{aligned} \]

This matches our original polynomial meaning we've found the correct zeros and everything works out.

When it comes to solving polynomials, no two polynomials will be solved the exact same way. It is important to use all of the tools that we outlined in this section to make the process as effective as possible, without having to draw the process out.
