CH 4.2 – Solving Systems by Substitution

Graphically solving systems of equations is only one option. Another option is to solve systems using Substitution. Each method leads itself to a particular way the equation is set up. While it doesn't really matter which method you use to solve systems of equations, it is still important to understand and be able to apply each method.

When solving a system of equations by substitution, you first take one of the equations, solve it for a variable, and then plug it into the other equation. The variable you solve for and the first equation you use don't really matter — try to select the equation that is simpler to solve for one variable or the other. It is also important that you check your solution to ensure the process was carried out correctly.

Linear Systems

Example

Solve each system of equations by the method of substitution.

  1. \(x + y = 4\)
    \(x + 2 = y\)
  2. \(2x + y = 5\)
    \(3x - 2y = 4\)
  3. \(2x - y = 10\)
    \(y = 2x - 4\)
  4. \(2x + 2y = 5\)
    \(3x = 7.5 - 3y\)

Solution

System 1

The second equation is already solved for \(y\). We substitute \(y = x + 2\) directly into the first equation and solve for \(x\):

\[\begin{aligned} x + \textcolor{red}{(x+2)} &= 4 \\ 2x + 2 &= 4 \\ 2x &= 2 \\ x &= 1 \end{aligned}\]

To find \(y\), plug \(x = 1\) back into the equation solved for \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned} \textcolor{red}{1} + 2 &= y \\ y &= 3 \end{aligned}\]
Solution: \((1,\,3)\)

Check in both original equations:

Check in Equation 2: \(x + 2 = y\)
\[\textcolor{red}{1} + 2 = \textcolor{blue}{3} \implies 3 = 3 \checkmark\]

True — this equation checks out.

Check in Equation 1: \(x + y = 4\)
\[\textcolor{red}{1} + \textcolor{blue}{3} = 4 \implies 4 = 4 \checkmark\]

True — this equation checks out as well.

System 2

Look for a variable with a coefficient of one. In the first equation, \(y\) can be isolated simply by moving \(2x\) to the other side: \(y = 5 - 2x\). Substitute into the second equation and solve for \(x\):

\[\begin{aligned} 3x - 2(\textcolor{red}{5-2x}) &= 4 \\ 3x - 10 + 4x &= 4 \\ 7x - 10 &= 4 \\ 7x &= 14 \\ x &= 2 \end{aligned}\]

Plug \(x = 2\) back into the equation solved for \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned} y &= 5 - 2(\textcolor{red}{2}) \\ y &= 5 - 4 \\ y &= 1 \end{aligned}\]
Solution: \((2,\,1)\) — verified correct in both equations. Consistent

System 3

The second equation is already solved for \(y\). Substitute \(y = 2x - 4\) into the first equation:

\[\begin{aligned} 2x - (\textcolor{red}{2x-4}) &= 10 \\ 2x - 2x + 4 &= 10 \\ 4 &\neq 10 \end{aligned}\]
The variable cancels and leaves a false statement. The left-hand and right-hand sides are not equal, which means this system has no solution — the lines are parallel and never intersect.

System 4

Of the two equations, the second is the closest to being solved for a variable. There is no rule that says we must solve for \(y\) — here we solve Equation 2 for \(x\):

\[\begin{aligned} 3x &= 7.5 - 3y \\ x &= 2.5 - y \end{aligned}\]

Substitute into Equation 1 and solve for \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned} 2(\textcolor{red}{2.5 - y}) + 2y &= 5 \\ 5 - 2y + 2y &= 5 \\ 5 &= 5 \end{aligned}\]

The variable cancels and leaves a true statement where both sides equal the same number. This means the system has infinite solutions — the two equations describe the same line.

In this course, saying "infinite solutions" is not enough — the answer must be expressed as an equation. We state the solution in terms of a chosen variable:

In terms of \(y\) (using \(x = 2.5 - y\) from above): \[\{(2.5 - y,\; y) \mid y \in \mathbb{R}\}\]

In terms of \(x\) (solving Equation 1 for \(y\) gives \(y = \tfrac{5}{2} - x\)): \[\left\{\left(x,\; \tfrac{5}{2} - x\right) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\]

You select a value for the free variable and both coordinates follow from the equation. We will cover this further in the next two sections.

Now notice how in this example we have a true statement where the left and right hand side of the equation both equal the same number. When this happens we have what is known as a infinite solutions.

Now, when you took algebra II in high school, it was ok to just say the answer was infinite solutions and be done with the problem. That is not the case in this course. Just because it is infinite solutions does not mean that we don't have an answer. We do, it is just in the form of an equation.

Nonlinear Systems

From time to time we will need to solve nonlinear systems of equations as well. Recall that these types of systems can have one solution, no solution, infinite solutions, or multiple solutions — it depends on the system. To solve nonlinear systems we take the same basic approach as with linear systems: solve one equation for a variable and plug it into the other equation.

Example

Solve the nonlinear systems using substitution.

  1. \(3x^2 + 4x - y = 7\)
    \(2x - y = -1\)
  2. \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\)
    \(-\dfrac{1}{2}x + y = -\dfrac{5}{2}\)

Solution

System 1: \(\;3x^2 + 4x - y = 7\) and \(2x - y = -1\)

Take the linear equation (Equation 2) and solve for \(y\), since it appears with a coefficient of one:

\[\begin{aligned} 2x - y &= -1 \\ y &= 2x + 1 \end{aligned}\]

Substitute into the first equation and solve for \(x\):

\[\begin{aligned} 3x^2 + 4x - (\textcolor{red}{2x+1}) &= 7 \\ 3x^2 + 4x - 2x - 1 &= 7 \\ 3x^2 + 2x - 1 &= 7 \\ 3x^2 + 2x - 8 &= 0 \\ (x+2)(3x-4) &= 0 \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} x + 2 &= 0 \\ x &= -2 \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} 3x - 4 &= 0 \\ x &= \frac{4}{3} \end{aligned}\]

Two \(x\)-values means two possible cases. Find the corresponding \(y\)-value for each using \(y = 2x + 1\):

Case 1: \(x = -2\)
\[\begin{aligned} y &= 2(\textcolor{red}{-2}) + 1 \\ y &= -4 + 1 \\ y &= -3 \end{aligned}\]

Solution: \((-2,\,-3)\)

Case 2: \(x = \dfrac{4}{3}\)
\[\begin{aligned} y &= 2\!\left(\textcolor{red}{\tfrac{4}{3}}\right) + 1 \\ y &= \frac{8}{3} + 1 \\ y &= \frac{11}{3} \end{aligned}\]

Solution: \(\left(\dfrac{4}{3},\,\dfrac{11}{3}\right)\)

This system has two solutions: \((-2,\,-3)\) and \(\left(\dfrac{4}{3},\,\dfrac{11}{3}\right)\). Graphically, the line intersects the parabola in two places. Consistent

System 2: \(\;x^2 + y^2 = 25\) and \(-\dfrac{1}{2}x + y = -\dfrac{5}{2}\)

To eliminate fractions, multiply both sides of Equation 2 by the LCM of the denominators, which is 2:

\[\begin{aligned} 2\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}x + y\right) &= 2\!\left(-\frac{5}{2}\right) \\ -x + 2y &= -5 \\ 2y &= x - 5 \\ x &= 2y + 5 \end{aligned}\]

Substitute \(x = 2y + 5\) into the first equation and solve for \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned} (\textcolor{red}{2y+5})^2 + y^2 &= 25 \\ 4y^2 + 20y + 25 + y^2 &= 25 \\ 5y^2 + 20y + 25 &= 25 \\ 5y^2 + 20y &= 0 \\ 5y(y + 4) &= 0 \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} 5y &= 0 \\ y &= 0 \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} y + 4 &= 0 \\ y &= -4 \end{aligned}\]

Two \(y\)-values means two possible cases. Find the corresponding \(x\)-value for each using \(x = 2y + 5\):

Case 1: \(y = 0\)
\[\begin{aligned} x &= 2(\textcolor{red}{0}) + 5 \\ x &= 5 \end{aligned}\]

Solution: \((5,\,0)\)

Case 2: \(y = -4\)
\[\begin{aligned} x &= 2(\textcolor{red}{-4}) + 5 \\ x &= -8 + 5 \\ x &= -3 \end{aligned}\]

Solution: \((-3,\,-4)\)

This system has two solutions: \((5,\,0)\) and \((-3,\,-4)\). Consistent

It is important to note that while we solved both quadratics above by factoring, it is possible you would need to use the quadratic formula or another method. It is also possible to obtain imaginary solutions — if that occurs, there is no real solution to the system, since we need a point that exists on the rectangular coordinate plane.
